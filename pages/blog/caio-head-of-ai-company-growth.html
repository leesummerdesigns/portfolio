CAIO, Head of AI and the Team Behind AI Growth

A practical guide to the responsibilities, skills and decisions that turn AI ambition into business results.

Louis Henrich · 6 min read

← Back to Blog

Hiring a Chief AI Officer can put AI on the executive agenda. It does not, by itself, turn a promising experiment into a product customers use or a process employees trust. That requires several kinds of leadership working together, with clear authority over funding, delivery, risk and adoption.

Titles such as CAIO and Head of AI are becoming more visible, but their meaning varies between companies. IBM’s overview explicitly describes overlapping responsibilities and different reporting lines. The useful starting point is therefore the mandate behind the title. Source: IBM’s overview of the Chief AI Officer role.

The division of responsibilities below is a proposed operating model for companies building their AI capability. It is a hiring and organisational design framework, rather than a universal definition of these jobs.

Chief AI Officer: decide where AI should create value

A CAIO should own the company-wide AI agenda: which business problems deserve investment, how different initiatives fit together and what level of risk leadership is willing to accept. The role needs authority to resolve competing priorities across departments and stop projects that no longer justify their cost.

This connects commercial judgement with technical understanding. IBM describes a combination of strategy, technology oversight, leadership and ethical judgement, with responsibilities that overlap those of the CIO, CTO and CDO. Source: IBM. The company still needs to decide how those boundaries work in practice.

What the position requires

Look for evidence of managing a portfolio of investments, influencing senior leaders and turning uncertain technical opportunities into credible business cases. A strong candidate can question model capability, data readiness and supplier dependency without pretending to replace every technical specialist. They should be comfortable discussing margins, customer needs, organisational change and failure scenarios in the same meeting.

Decision rights: propose and govern the AI investment portfolio, set common priorities with business leaders, and escalate risk decisions through the appropriate executive process. Finance should validate the business case; legal, security and other control functions retain their own responsibilities.

Success measures: realised business value, adoption of funded capabilities, time to justified investment decisions and the proportion of projects scaled or stopped on evidence. Counting pilots rewards activity without showing whether anything improved.

Head of AI: make the strategy work in production

The Head of AI should own the delivery capability: the people, engineering practices and operating discipline needed to build or integrate dependable AI systems. In a smaller company, this person may also set strategy. In a larger organisation, they might report to the CAIO, CTO or another technology leader. The reporting line matters less than agreement on who controls resources and who approves releases.

What the position requires

Look for experience shipping and operating systems with real users. That includes data pipelines, model selection, evaluation, software integration, security and monitoring. For generative AI, candidates should understand retrieval, tool permissions, failure testing and how quality changes when a model or prompt changes. They need enough product judgement to challenge a technically impressive solution that solves the wrong problem.

Ask candidates to walk through a production failure. How was it detected? What was rolled back? Which evaluation was missing? What changed afterwards? This reveals more than a list of frameworks on a CV.

Decision rights: choose implementation approaches within the agreed architecture and budget, establish technical release criteria, staff the delivery team and halt a release that fails those criteria.

Success measures: useful systems running reliably, quality against agreed evaluations, cost per successful task, recovery time and predictable delivery. A good demonstration is an early milestone; sustainable operation is the continuing responsibility.

The roles that connect leadership to daily work

A CAIO and a Head of AI need colleagues who can translate ambition into workflows. Depending on company size, these can be dedicated jobs or explicit responsibilities within existing positions.

AI Product Manager

Mandate: decide which user problem to solve and what successful behaviour looks like. Requirements: product discovery, domain knowledge, experimentation, evaluation design and an understanding of AI uncertainty. This person defines acceptable errors and human handoffs with users, engineers and control functions. Growth contribution: better activation, retention or conversion, measured against a baseline and balanced with quality.

AI Governance or Responsible AI Lead

Mandate: organise risk assessment, documentation, review and incident escalation. Requirements: the ability to translate legal and policy requirements into checks delivery teams can execute, alongside technical literacy and stakeholder judgement. Growth contribution: clearer approvals and stronger evidence for customers and procurement teams. This person should be able to challenge a deployment independently of its delivery targets.

NIST’s voluntary AI Risk Management Framework provides a useful reference through its Govern, Map, Measure and Manage functions. These are continuing responsibilities across the lifecycle, not four boxes to tick before launch. Source: NIST AI RMF Playbook.

AI Platform or ML Engineering Lead

Mandate: provide reliable shared infrastructure for data access, evaluation, deployment and monitoring. Requirements: software and data engineering, operational reliability, access control and cost management. Growth contribution: teams can reuse proven components and spend less time rebuilding the same foundations. Measure deployment lead time and operational quality alongside infrastructure spend.

AI Adoption and Enablement Lead

Mandate: help people change how work gets done. Requirements: learning design, process improvement, employee engagement and the ability to distinguish a training problem from a poorly designed tool. Growth contribution: sustained use that improves outcomes. Track successful task completion and time released for valuable work, rather than licence allocation or training attendance alone.

Write down the decisions before drawing the organisation chart

Deloitte’s technology leadership research identifies fragmentation and misaligned funding as obstacles to execution. More executive titles can increase coordination demands unless authority is made explicit. Source: Deloitte’s 2026 technology leadership analysis.

A workable arrangement gives the CAIO responsibility for the portfolio, the Head of AI responsibility for technical delivery, and a business owner responsibility for the resulting operational outcome. The CTO or CIO agrees architecture and integration boundaries; the CDO or data owner agrees data access and quality; security, privacy and legal teams review the matters within their remit.

Make release, exception and shutdown authority explicit. If everyone assumes someone else can stop a failing system, the organisation has a gap regardless of how senior the job titles sound.

How these positions can support growth

Consider a hypothetical company introducing an AI assistant for customer onboarding. The CAIO sponsors the investment because slow onboarding limits capacity. The product manager identifies the steps customers struggle with. The Head of AI and platform team build and evaluate the assistant. Governance reviews its data use and boundaries. The adoption lead helps support staff handle the new workflow, while the business owner remains accountable for onboarding performance.

The team compares onboarding completion, support demand and customer satisfaction against the existing process. It includes model fees, integration, human review and ongoing support in the cost calculation. A faster interaction only creates value if it leads to a better overall outcome.

There is a real organisational precedent for connecting central AI expertise with business teams. In its 2022 account of its AI programme, Schneider Electric described a global hub-and-spoke model working with business units on customer and operational use cases. That illustrates a structure; it does not establish that every company needs to copy it or that a title alone causes growth. Source: Schneider Electric.

Choose the structure for the stage of the company

Early adoption: an existing executive sponsor, a capable delivery lead and a committed business owner may be enough. Give them access to specialist advice where needed.

Several teams scaling AI: a Head of AI can coordinate delivery standards and shared capabilities. Product ownership should stay close to the customers and processes being improved.

AI central to the business model: a CAIO can be valuable when investment choices and risks regularly cross business units and require executive authority. Appointing one should resolve a real coordination problem.

For the first 90 days, ask the leadership team to produce a baseline of current use, a small prioritised portfolio, named decision owners and one measured delivery with explicit scale-or-stop criteria. These are suggested management deliverables, not a promise that every deployment can be completed in three months.

The lasting contribution of these roles will be the capability they leave across the company: teams that can select useful applications, test them honestly, operate them reliably and change direction when the evidence says they should. That is a credible foundation for growth.
